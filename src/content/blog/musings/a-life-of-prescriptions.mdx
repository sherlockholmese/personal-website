In one's life, many things are prescribed to us — our parents prescribe to us basic morals and values, our society prescribes human necessities such as education as a means to sustenance, and countless other individuals try to convince us what is "good" and what is "bad". And of course, there is nothing wrong with heeding the advice of other people. Developing proper moral frameworks is a fundamental step towards being able to make rational decisions. Being (somewhat) educated is quite a fundamental requirement to find a job and sustain oneself financially. Unfortunately, the land of prescriptions has crept towards areas that are quite personal to many: one's own perspectives and opinions.

# To argumentate.

In English language education in Singapore, it is inevitable that one has to learn how to argumentate as part of the subject. It is part and parcel of the application of English after all — the ability to express substantives in languages of communication is quite fundamental to our social experiences. However, as Singaporeans try to *optimise the joy out of every single probable aspect of life*, in their attempt to optimise the realm of thinking itself, we have ended up producing agents that are rarely capable of producing original thought.

To see this in action we just have to take a look at the English (and General Paper) tuition industry at large. Many of these centres advertise topic packages, where the formulation of opinions and ideas regarding *the majority of our aspects of life* have been nicely compiled for consumers (i.e. students of the Singapore education system) to memorise and apply during papers. In our attempt to optimise the area of critical thinking, we prescribe pre-formulated thoughts for students to utilise during examinations, ensuring consistently high quality opinions and ideas (?) are being expressed. After all, the markers of these examination papers cannot place credit on anyone for the thought; how are they supposed to know if these opinions and ideas were pre-formulated in advance, or truly thought up as part of a sudent's cognitive thinking during the examination? However, these methods rob our learners the actual experience of thinking up these substantives themselves.

From a student's point of view, such a prescription is wonderful! Not only do I not have to bear the stressful experience of formulating opinions during the paper, but I am also able to buy my way to resources that provide this thinking for me. Alas, this trap is one that many have fallen into — by abstracting away the need to formulate their own ideas and perspectives, these individuals will become agents that have no idea on what they think, nor who they are themselves. As such, we observe the presence of students that are able to argue beautifully in prose deeply intellectual topics such as science and ethics, international relations, etc., but are unable to form their own perspectives on anything that are beyond what they are prescribed to consider.

This idea of pushing the responsbility of thinking to those that are able to "think better than us" is dangerous, almost analogous to what some might refer as *brainwashing*. Perhaps this is why political apathy is such a big issue in Singapore. As cautious, responsibility-avoiding individuals who wish to walk the trodden path, we fall back on adopting whatever ideas we think "smarter people have already more thoroughly thought about" instead of forming our own; for bearing the burden of proof to our own argument is something that not many of us are comfortable with. Because this is the easy way out.
